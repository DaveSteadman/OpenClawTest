## 1. Introduction

The purpose of this report is to analyse recent news stories concerning radar products from major vendors and to identify the five most significant product differences among their offerings. The analysis draws on publicly available announcements dated February 2026, covering BAE Systems, Lockheed Martin, Raytheon, and Saab. The findings highlight distinct technical attributes, platform integrations, market focus, contract scale, and emerging opportunities for differentiation or entry into new markets.

## 2. Product Landscape Overview

BAE Systems secured a £453.5 million contract to deliver 38‑40 European Common Radar System Mk2 (ECRS Mk2) AESA radars for the Royal Air Force’s Eurofighter Typhoon fleet, emphasizing electronic‑warfare (EW) capability and multi‑target tracking in contested air environments.

Lockheed Martin announced the delivery of the first Sentinel A4 radar from Low‑Rate Initial Production Lot 2 to the U.S. Army, a 360‑degree active electronically scanned array (AESA) designed for ground‑based air and missile defence, with a focus on counter‑UAS, cruise‑missile and rotary‑wing threat detection.

Saab’s product line includes the lightweight, X‑band Giraffe 1X short‑range 3D radar (under a US$46 million contract) and the larger Giraffe 4A multi‑function radar (a SEK 1.4 billion order for the Swedish Armed Forces). The 1X emphasizes rapid 1‑second refresh, portability and drone discrimination, while the 4A provides up to 400 km range, eight updates per second and software‑based upgrades.

Raytheon, operating under RTX, was selected by DARPA to develop an advanced maritime sensing and targeting system that integrates electro‑optical/infrared (EO/IR) sensors, AI‑driven detection software and command‑and‑control capabilities for commercial and naval logistics vessels. Although not a traditional radar, the system represents a complementary sensing approach for maritime security.

## 3. Top Five Product Differences

1. **Frequency Band and Scan Architecture** – BAE’s ECRS Mk2 is a high‑power, multi‑mode AESA radar optimized for fighter‑jet air‑to‑air and air‑to‑ground missions, whereas Lockheed’s Sentinel A4 is a 360‑degree AESA intended for ground‑based, all‑around surveillance. Saab’s Giraffe 1X uses an X‑band active array tuned for short‑range, rapid‑refresh detection, while the Giraffe 4A expands to longer‑range, multi‑function operation with up to eight refresh cycles per second. Raytheon’s DARPA system relies on EO/IR rather than radio frequency, offering a non‑RF complementary detection layer for maritime targets.

2. **Platform Integration** – BAE’s radar is integrated onto the Eurofighter Typhoon, a high‑performance combat aircraft, highlighting airborne EW and sensor fusion. Lockheed’s Sentinel is mounted on ground vehicles and integrates with the U.S. Army’s Integrated Air and Missile Defense Battle Command System. Saab’s 1X is portable for rooftop, mast, or small‑boat deployment; the 4A is mobile for ground‑based air surveillance and can be navalised. Raytheon’s solution is designed for tethered drones and semi‑autonomous platforms towed by commercial ships.

3. **Market Focus and Customer Base** – BAE primarily serves the United Kingdom’s Ministry of Defence and NATO air‑defence missions. Lockheed targets the U.S. Army’s land‑based air‑defence market. Saab operates across multiple geographies, supplying the U.S. Army (partner‑capacity program), the Swedish Armed Forces, and expanding production capacity in the United Kingdom. Raytheon’s DARPA contract positions it in the emerging commercial maritime‑security market and U.S. Navy communications (via Link 16 radios).

4. **Contract Scale and Production Volume** – BAE’s £453 million award covers up to 40 ECRS Mk2 units, sustaining roughly 1,300 UK jobs. Lockheed’s Sentinel programme has delivered the first of 19 radars and is moving toward full‑rate production. Saab’s Giraffe 1X contract is valued at US$46 million, while the Giraffe 4A order totals SEK 1.4 billion (approximately US$144 million) with deliveries through 2027. Raytheon’s DARPA award does not disclose monetary size but represents a high‑visibility research‑to‑prototype effort.

5. **Technological Differentiators** – BAE’s ECRS Mk2 incorporates built‑in EW functions and multi‑mode operation for contested environments. Lockheed’s Sentinel A4 emphasizes 360‑degree coverage, integration with FAAD‑C2 and advanced counter‑UAS algorithms. Saab’s Giraffe 1X provides a dedicated Drone Tracker that reduces false alarms, while the Giraffe 4A offers software‑based updates and the ability to focus on known threat sectors with up to eight refreshes per second. Raytheon’s maritime system leverages AI‑driven detection across EO/IR sensors, delivering real‑time target tracking for unmanned surface vehicles and other emerging threats.

## 4. Market Analysis and Opportunities

The current market segmentation shows clear gaps where vendors can expand. BAE could leverage its EW‑rich ECRS Mk2 technology to enter the ground‑based air‑defence segment, offering a high‑performance AESA for mobile Army platforms, thereby diversifying beyond the UK air‑force niche.

Lockheed Martin’s Sentinel A4, while already positioned for land‑based defence, could be adapted for naval surface‑ship or littoral combat vessel installations, capitalising on the growing demand for 360‑degree maritime surveillance against hypersonic and drone threats.

Saab’s modular Giraffe family demonstrates strong portability and rapid‑refresh capabilities. By integrating AI‑based classification (similar to Raytheon’s approach) into the Giraffe 1X and 4A suites, Saab could enhance autonomous threat discrimination, opening doors to export markets focused on low‑cost, high‑density air‑defence networks in the Indo‑Pacific and Africa.

Raytheon’s DARPA maritime sensing system, though not a radar, highlights a trend toward multi‑sensor fusion for commercial shipping protection. Partnering with radar manufacturers to embed a compact AESA (e.g., a scaled‑down Giraffe 1X) could create a hybrid radar‑EO/IR package, differentiating Raytheon in the civilian maritime security arena.

All four vendors operate within overlapping NATO and U.S. defence ecosystems. Joint development of interoperable data‑link standards (e.g., extending Link 16 capabilities demonstrated by BAE’s MIDS JTRS radios) could create a unified situational‑awareness layer, offering a competitive advantage in integrated air‑and‑maritime battlespace management.

## 5. Summary

The analysis of February 2026 news reveals distinct product philosophies among BAE Systems, Lockheed Martin, Saab and Raytheon. Key differences lie in frequency band and scan architecture, platform integration, target markets, contract scale, and unique technological features such as built‑in EW, 360‑degree coverage, rapid‑refresh drone tracking, and AI‑driven sensor fusion. Opportunities exist for BAE to move into ground‑based radar, for Lockheed to pursue naval variants, for Saab to embed AI for enhanced classification, and for Raytheon to combine radar with its EO/IR maritime system. Collaborative standards development, especially around data‑link interoperability, could further differentiate each vendor while expanding their reach into emerging defence and commercial security markets.
